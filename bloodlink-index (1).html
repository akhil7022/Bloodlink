Donation awareness

Know before you go.

Most people who want to donate are turned away by a detail they could have checked in thirty seconds. Here is that thirty seconds.

What actually happens

01
Screening, 10 minutes
A short questionnaire and a finger-prick test for haemoglobin.
02
The donation, 8–12 minutes
One unit — about 450 ml. You are reclined the whole time.
03
Rest and refreshments, 15 minutes
Juice, biscuits, and a nurse watching for dizziness.
04
Testing and release, 24 hours
Every unit is screened for HIV, hepatitis B, hepatitis C, syphilis and malaria before use.
05
You recover in 24–48 hours
Plasma volume returns within a day. Red cells take a few weeks.
Myth 01 Donating makes you weak for weeks.
False. You lose about 450 ml — roughly 8% of your blood volume — and your body replaces the fluid within 24 hours. Most donors are back at a desk the same afternoon, just advised against heavy lifting for the rest of the day.
Myth 02 It hurts and the needle is huge.
Mostly false. You feel a brief pinch at insertion. The needle is larger than a vaccine needle because red cells are fragile and must not be squeezed — but the sensation lasts seconds, not minutes.
Myth 03 I am on medication, so I can never donate.
Depends entirely on the drug. Common medications for blood pressure, thyroid, cholesterol and asthma are usually fine. Antibiotics and blood thinners typically require a waiting period. The screening questionnaire exists precisely for this — never self-reject.
Myth 04 You can catch a disease from donating.
False. Every needle and bag is sterile and single-use, opened in front of you. There is no pathway for infection from donating.
Myth 05 Rare groups are the only ones in demand.
False. O− and O+ carry the highest routine demand because O− is the universal red-cell donor used in emergencies before a patient's group is known. Common groups are the supply backbone.
